Preferential voting — the lower house

Every lower house seat in Victoria is decided by full preferential voting. This is also called "instant run-off" voting. It's not first-past-the-post — the candidate with the most "1"s doesn't automatically win.

The rule

A candidate wins when they have more than 50% of the vote. If no one has more than 50% on first preferences, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated and their voters' next preferences are distributed. This repeats until someone passes 50%.

A worked example

Imagine the district of Carlton has four candidates and 10,000 formal votes. Here's a plausible ballot:

3Alice (Liberal)
1Bob (Greens)
4Carla (Labor)
2Dinesh (Independent)

This voter wants Bob first, Dinesh second, Alice third, Carla last. Every box is numbered, so the vote is formal.

Count 1 — first preferences

CandidateVotesShare
Alice (Liberal)3,80038%
Bob (Greens)3,20032%
Carla (Labor)2,50025%
Dinesh (Independent)5005%

Alice is in front but nobody has 50%+. Dinesh is last, so he's eliminated.

Count 2 — Dinesh's preferences flow

Every ballot that had Dinesh as "1" is now re-counted using the "2" on that ballot. Say they split 300 → Bob, 150 → Alice, 50 → Carla.

CandidateNew total
Alice3,950
Bob3,500
Carla2,550

Still no 50%+. Carla is now last — eliminated.

Count 3 — Carla's preferences flow

Each of Carla's 2,550 ballots goes to whoever that voter put next (skipping Dinesh, already eliminated). Say they split 1,800 → Alice, 750 → Bob.

CandidateFinalShare
Alice (Liberal)5,75057.5%
Bob (Greens)4,25042.5%

Alice wins — she's over 50% in the final two-candidate-preferred count.

Key implications

  • Your "1" never gets wasted. If your first choice is eliminated, your vote simply flows to your next choice.
  • A candidate can come second (or even third) on primary votes and still win, if enough preferences flow their way.
  • How-to-vote cards suggest a preference order — but you decide, every box.

What's a "two-party-preferred" number?

On election night, commentators talk about "two-party-preferred" (TPP or 2PP). It's the final-round tally specifically between Labor and the Coalition, computed by distributing all other candidates' preferences between those two. The example above ends in a two-candidate-preferred (TCP) count — Alice (Liberal) 57.5% vs Bob (Greens) 42.5% — because Carla (Labor) was eliminated earlier. A TPP figure for this seat would be reported as a separate notional Alice-vs-Carla count.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving a box blank. Your ballot is informal and doesn't count.
  • Writing the same number twice. Same problem — informal.
  • A "donkey vote" — numbering 1,2,3,… straight down the paper. It's a formal vote but may not reflect your actual preferences, since candidate order on the paper is random.
